\subsection*{A preliminary matched multi-model intervention (E5)}

The controlled results establish that the seven applicability invariants are
executable and causally separable (E1--E4); they do not show that
\emph{prompting} a language model for an explicit situation state improves it.
As a first, deliberately bounded test of that E5 claim, three Gemini models
answer a stratified, evidence-complete sample of SituationCatch-Bench
(30 items per category, $n=210$) with evidence, decoding and budget held fixed,
varying only the prompt: a plain \emph{direct} answer, a \emph{structured} JSON
prompt or an explicit \emph{situation}-state prompt (Methods). The primary
quantity is the within-model paired situation$-$structured contrast; the run
passes a completion audit with no failed calls.

\input{section/tables/e5_stats_table}

The result is null-to-negative (Table~\ref{tab:e5-stats}). Prompting for an
explicit situation state never yields a cluster-bootstrap interval indicating
an improvement over a matched structured prompt:
the paired situation$-$structured differences are $+1.4$, $-10.0$ and $+0.0$
percentage points, with a $95\%$ cluster-bootstrap interval that excludes zero
only for \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-lite}, where the situation prompt is
\emph{worse} under this analysis. The intervals are otherwise wide because the
benchmark has only seven template families, so resampling categories -- the
honest unit of independence -- leaves little power for a directional claim.
The situation and structured conditions also use roughly three times the total
provider-reported tokens and have substantially higher median latency than the
direct prompt, with no accuracy gain and no reduction in selective risk.

A small natural-language check points the same way. On a 12-item sample from the
public SituatedQA temporal split, the two date-anchored conditions show no
advantage for the situation prompt over a structured one (descriptive
differences of $-16.7$, $+0.0$ and $+0.0$ points across the three models). At
$n=12$ this is a smoke test, not a result.

A retrieval-augmented baseline sharpens the picture (Extended Data
Table~\ref{tab:e5-rag}). Answering from only the top-3 lexically retrieved
claims, without situation metadata, is the strongest condition overall
($91.3\%$ versus $83.5\%$ structured and $80.6\%$ situation): a focused,
relevant subset beats all claims plus a state instruction. Descriptively, the
exception is the two categories that most require reasoning over the
applicability state -- temporal validity and observer knowledge -- where the
full-evidence situation condition beats RAG ($+8.9$ and $+5.6$ points); given
category-level multiplicity this pattern is exploratory, not confirmatory.
Where applicability depends on which claim is valid \emph{now} or is known to
\emph{this} observer, retrieving the most similar text is not enough. A
tool-using agent that issues its own searches over the item's claims does not
close this gap: it is the weakest condition overall ($77.5\%$) and drops
furthest on temporal ($64.4\%$) and observer ($66.7\%$) items, because
self-directed retrieval can still fetch the most similar claim rather than the
applicable one.

We read this as motivating the paper's distinction rather than as a plug-in
prompting win: a prompt-level ``situation'' field is not situation awareness.
Consistent with the controlled finding that accuracy collapses once states must
be \emph{sensed} from text ($100.0\%\rightarrow77.9\%$), the bottleneck is
situation sensing and validation, not a state slot in the prompt. This
single-provider, $n=210$ pilot neither confirms nor refutes the hypothesis at
scale; a confirmatory E5 requires multiple model families, a larger
natural-language set and human agreement (Methods).
